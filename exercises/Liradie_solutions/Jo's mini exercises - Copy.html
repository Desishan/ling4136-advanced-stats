Exercise 1: What changes when a model becomes Bayesian?

  • In your own words, explain what people usually mean when they say they “turn a frequentist model into a Bayesian model.”

They are starting with a simpler linear model, which may be generalised and/or heirarchical, and using the same formula to run a model through a Bayesian engine. The Bayesian model requires additional information in the form of a prior distribution for each value in the formula, and for each output, instead of returning a single value it runs many iterations of the modelling procedure, returning a distribution of possible outputs. These distributions can be compared with a null hypothesis, which may be point zero (like a frequentist model) but may also be a different point or a whole interval, called a ROPE. This comparison can be done with the Savage-Dickey density ratio, which determines how much the data favors the likelihood of the postierior distribution being outside the null hypothesis, compared to the prior. This calculation returns a Bayes Factor, which can be interpreted in terms of levels of uncertainty, making the result more informative than a frequentist model, which only returns a comparison to point null.

  • Identify the roles of the likelihood, the prior, and the posterior.

I’m not sure what ‘likelihood’ means here.

The prior for a given parameter is a distribution of plausible values we think the parameter could have, given the information we have before seeing the data.

The posterior for the same parameter is a distribution of plausible values the model predicts for it, having seen both the priors and the data.

Exercise 5: SESOI

  • In your own words, define SESOI.

    SESOI stands for smallest effect size of interest, and is the amount of change to the response variable the researcher decides a predictor must affect for it to be a ‘meaningful’ effect.

  • Explain the difference between statistical significance and practical importance.

    Statistical significance is typically calculated in comparison to zero, i.e. if it’s sufficiently unlikely that an effect is zero it is considered statistically significant. Practical importance depends on whether the effect is of a size that actually ‘matters’ in the world.

  • Explain the difference between a point null of exactly 0 and an interval null around 0.

    An interval null around zero describes an area where an effect in either direction would be small enough no be considered equivalent to zero.

  • A posterior for a treatment effect is centered at 0.03, and your SESOI is [-0.10, 0.10]. What is the first practical question you should ask?

    What is the spread of the posterior? Is any of it outside the ROPE? If so, what proportion compared to the prior?

  • A researcher says, “Any effect above zero matters.” What is wrong with that claim?

    If the predicted effect is too small to be measurable with the research equipment used, it’s meaningless; if it’s too small to have practical implications in the world, it’s close to meaningless.

  • Why is SESOI always tied to a scale, a context, and a decision problem?

    I don’t know this formulation, but broadly all of these things decide what an effect may mean in the world; some contexts/decisions are predicated on stronger effects than others.

Exercise 6: Scale matters

  • Suppose you are modeling reaction times in milliseconds. Compare the practical meaning of the following two priors on class = “sd”:

    1. cauchy(0, 3)

      The prior is quite precise, predicting an effect with very little variation.

    2. cauchy(0, 100)

      The prior is quite broad, predicting an effect which could vary a lot.

  • Discuss what each prior says before seeing the data, and why one might be too narrow or too diffuse depending on the scale of the outcome.

    The narrow prior may make the outcome seem very powerful if an outcome is measured with a mean of, say, 10ms, as a large mass of data would be away from the prior.

    The diffuse prior may make the outcome seem not very powerful/meaningful even if it’s mean is something like 50ms, because that’s fully within the prior.

Exercise 7: Weakly or informative priors

  • What is a weakly informative prior?

    A weakly informative prior diffusely covers an area of values that are conceivable based on the information available. In the case of effects, it’s generally centred on zero.

  • Why is “as flat as possible” often not the best strategy?

    Flat priors reproduce the uninformed prediction in frequentist models, overly favoring values that are absolutely inconceivable. This makes real data values seem more meaningful in comparison.

  • Give one example of how a weakly informative prior can help with estimation, interpretation, or computation.

    A weakly informative prior can be used to compute a Bayes factor, as a baseline of conceivable values to compare posteriors to.

Exercise 9: Reading results against a SESOI

  • Suppose an effect estimate is 0.18 with a 95% credible interval of [0.02, 0.31], and the SESOI is [- 0.10, 0.10]. Write 3-4 sentences interpreting this result from a practical-importance perspective.

    While this effect is very likely positive, a little less than 1/3 of effect’s posterior is inside the ROPE, so there’s a reasonable likelihood that it is not practically meaningful based on the rationale of the SESOI choice. It is more likely than not that it is practically meaningful, but not proven.

  • Now suppose an effect estimate is 0.05 with a 95% credible interval of [-0.02, 0.12], with the same SESOI. What is uncertain here? Be precise.

    It is uncertain whether there is an effect at all, as the 95% CI overlaps with zero, and whether the effect is practically meaningful. There is a plausible, but relatively unlikely case (a little under 1/5 of the posterior) that there is a positive, practically meaningful effect.

  • Now suppose an effect estimate is 0.01 with a 95% credible interval of [-0.03, 0.04], again with the same SESOI. What would be the most defensible conclusion?

    The most defensible conclusion is that there is no practically important effect.

Exercise 12: Sensitivity analysis

  • Imagine you fit the same model twice. In Model A you use a narrow prior on class = “sd”; in Model B you use a much wider prior. The posterior intervals for the main effects are noticeably wider in Model B.

  • Give two possible interpretations of this result.

    1. Model B captures more of the actual variation among participants and items, and reflects that this means the main effects cannot be predicted so consistently.
    2. Model B predicts more variation among participants and items than there actually is, artificially making the prediction less precise.

  • Then explain what you would report in a write-up to show that you took prior sensitivity seriously.

    The spread of the predicted effect distribution is noticeably affected by the researcher’s choices of priors. Outcomes should be interpreted with reference to the priors used in the input.
